The Weights Walk Out

The security standard middle powers need before hosting frontier AI

Host-government security proto-standards

Our project aims to produce well-evidenced, expert-endorsed "proto-standards" on what host governments must have in place before frontier AI is deployed on their soil. The lack of such endorsed standards is a significant blocker to trusted access: labs and partner governments have no shared basis for judging whether a middle power can host frontier AI securely, and existing frameworks such as RAND's model weight security levels stop at the lab. We are aiming to convene experts from frontier labs, AI security institutes, think tanks, standard developers and middle-power governments to develop concrete, actionable recommendations on specific issues. It would use RAND's framework as the baseline, update the recommendations periodically as technology, threats and best practices evolve, and feed gaps back to standard developers. Host governments could voluntarily adopt the proto-standards to earn trusted access, while labs and partner governments could draw on or point to them when deciding where frontier models are hosted and on what terms. Once proto-standards are endorsed, our project would provide technical assistance so host governments can meet them through their own laws and institutions. This explainer walks through our strategy and theory of change, the risks the proto-standards address, what they would cover, how a government would use them under pressure, and how they could be adopted.

The risk

Safety impact

Frontier AI will increasingly be hosted in middle powers. Hosting brings three risks that a host government, not a lab, is best placed to manage.

Risk 1

Theft of model weights and algorithms

Exfiltration of model weights or proprietary algorithms through technical, human (insider) or cyber routes, by nation-states or malicious actors.

The proto-standard: vetting, insider-threat programs, inspection authority, security-conditioned licensing and a clear offence.
Risk 2

Chip smuggling

Controlled AI chips diverted through transit hubs using front companies, false end-user papers and payment trails, where domestic law, investigators and prosecutors are not equipped to stop it.

The proto-standard: AI-chip red flags, usable offences and a way to act on leads.
Risk 3

Incidents with no warning

An unexpected or dangerous AI event, such as a hosted model used in a cyberattack, with no agreed way to raise a rapid alert across borders.

The proto-standard: reporting within hours, one lead agency and a tested channel to partners.

Middle powers can gain leverage by offering security in exchange for access to frontier models, but only if they have adequate standards, trust and enforcement. Labs and partner governments increasingly treat security as the price of access: the more trusted a host, the higher the tier of access it gets (Leicht, 80,000 Hours). Today's standards set what labs and data center operators must do. Nothing yet says what a host government must have in place.

Why this is a catastrophic risk

Once frontier weights leave a secure site, every safeguard the developer built can be stripped out: safety training removed, the model fine-tuned for cyberattacks or biological weapons design, and copies run anywhere, with no way to recall them. Diverted chips do the same for capability itself: they let actors train frontier-scale models outside any testing, monitoring or treaty, which undermines the compute-based agreements most proposals for pacing frontier AI rely on. A single leak is irreversible, so prevention has to happen before hosting, not after.

So what for safetyEvery frontier model or chip that leaks through a hub ends up outside any safety or verification regime. Security is the prerequisite for hosting frontier AI safely and for trusted access.

How this works
  • Each shipment uses one evasion trick. Each measure is designed to catch one kind of trick at one checkpoint. A shipment stops at the first checkpoint where a matching measure is switched on.
  • Two catches exist today without any new measure: a false final destination can be caught by existing destination declarations, and one shipment is caught after delivery through a tip-off, as in a real case.
  • One shipment (an insider at a legitimate buyer) gets through even with every measure on. No standard is perfect; the point is how many are stopped, and how early.
  • The tricks are illustrative patterns from the cited cases, not data. The counts are not probabilities.

So what for safetyToday, most diverted chips get through a hub, and the ones that are caught are caught late. With the proto-standard, most are stopped before they leave, while there is still time to keep frontier compute inside a safety regime.

The problem

Hosts cannot yet protect the frontier AI on their soil

No data center meets SL4-SL5; up to 1.6 million H100-equivalents smuggled through 2025; no agency clearly owns AI incidents; lab-side frameworks stop at the lab.

Who it affects

Host and transit governments first

Direct: security, regulatory and justice agencies; customs and financial intelligence units. Indirect: labs, partner governments, global society.

The story

An insider copies weights from a foreign-run site

Today: no vetting, no access, no alert; access is cut. With the standard: a report within hours, a case under local law, partners alerted.

The approach

Endorse, then implement

An outside advocate in the style of FATF or Transparency International, not an auditor. Each country uses its own laws. Neutral on buildout, with published no-go conditions.

Inputs

What goes in

Convenings, technical security partners, AI security institutes, financial intelligence unit networks, RAND and the SL5 Task Force.

Theory of victory · what winning looks like

The win condition

  1. A shared, expert-endorsed host-government security proto-standard exists, and labs and partner governments refer to it when deciding where frontier models are hosted.
  2. At least one major hub visibly meets it, and hosting there is seen as safe rather than a theft route.
  3. AI-chip diversion through hubs is routinely detected and prosecuted under local law.
  4. A serious AI incident triggers a report within hours and partner alerts, not misattribution between rivals.
  5. Because middle powers can verify and enforce, future international agreements to pace or limit frontier AI become enforceable.

Why the actors converge

  • Host governments want trusted access and investment.
  • Labs want hosting options without theft risk.
  • Partner governments want diversion stopped without running enforcement abroad.
  • Security becomes the price of access, so it is in everyone's interest.

Signposts

Winning

  • Labs or partner governments cite the proto-standard.
  • A host designates a lead agency.
  • A prosecution uses the new tools.
  • A tabletop is run jointly by 2+ countries.

Losing

  • Access is decided by political deals with no security conditions.
  • A theft is traced to a hosted site.
  • Standards are seen as Washington's demands rather than shared ones.

Assumptions

  • Host demand: at least two governments want readiness support and will share information in confidence.
  • Acceptance: labs and partner governments accept host-implemented standards as meaningful.
  • Technical depth: a technical security partner covers model-security controls.
  • Access politics: access deals and carveouts for trusted allies continue.
  • Neutral standing: hosts see the effort as an outside advocate, not Washington's enforcer.

Cost to the government

Qualitative, and modest:

  • Regulatory amendments to existing laws: data center licensing scope, a prescribed incident type, a weights offence.
  • A designated lead agency for AI incidents with catastrophic potential.
  • Training for customs, police, prosecutors and financial intelligence analysts.
  • Reporting rules and a partner contact chain.
  • No new infrastructure.

What many hubs already have

  • Export-control laws covering transshipment and brokering.
  • Fraud and money-laundering laws that can reach false end-user claims, as Singapore's 2025-2026 prosecutions show (BBC).
  • Cyber incident reporting for critical infrastructure.
  • Data center licensing, often new, as with Singapore's 2026 Digital Infrastructure Bill (MDDI).
  • AI safety institutes and financial intelligence units.

Risks and mitigations

  • Seen as enforcing for Washington. Own-law framing, host co-chairs and co-authored outputs.
  • "More hosting means more theft surface." Published no-go conditions, so an assessment can say a site is not ready.
  • Technical credibility. Partner with model-security experts for the technical layer.
  • Access to security services. Work through AI security institutes, interior and justice ministries and financial intelligence units.

Could this really happen?Yes. The step up is mainly amendments, designations and training on top of laws many hubs already have, and every actor gets something it already wants: trusted access, safer hosting, or diversion stopped.

About and sources

Clarity

Snapshot

This page reflects public sources available as of 30 October 2026, published as a November 2026 snapshot. Several cited cases are ongoing.

The scenarios are hypothetical

The game's scenarios are invented. No real lab, operator or company is depicted as a victim or culprit.

How Singapore's laws were rated

In place: a law or institution clearly meets the requirement. Partial: something exists but does not fully cover frontier weights or AI chips. Gap: sources show nothing meets it. Unclear: we could not confirm either way. Ratings use public sources only, including law-firm summaries, and are not legal advice.

How the interactives work

The shipment run, the game and the coalition builder run on simple, visible rules listed with each one. They illustrate how the requirements interact; they are not predictions or probabilities.

Key debates

Align with Washington or hedge?Some analysts argue middle powers should align closely with the U.S. to keep access; others argue that pressure to pick a bloc makes hedging more rational.
Is more frontier capacity in more countries good?Balance-of-power arguments say yes; others see more actors and more copies of weights as more risk.
Who sets security standards?Some expect the U.S. to set them with carveouts for trusted allies; others note that buyers resist conditions set by one supplier country.
Do export controls reduce risk?Some see enforcement as part of a race; others see it as the first step of any deterrence or treaty regime; some doubt controls slow the frontier at all.
What are AI summits for?Some want summits to deliver incident reporting and verification; others think they suit only information sharing and narrow deals.
Is verification near or far?Verifying great-power commitments is widely seen as hard; verifying a host's domestic compliance is a lower bar that may be reachable now.
