The Weights Walk Out

The security standard middle powers need before hosting frontier AI

Garg and Sussman
Snapshot, November 2026 · For peer and judges review
Scroll to begin ↓
Host-government security proto-standards

Our project aims to produce well-evidenced, expert-endorsed "proto-standards" on what host governments must have in place before frontier AI is deployed on their soil. The lack of endorsed standards is a significant blocker to trusted access: labs and partner governments have no shared basis for judging whether a middle power can host frontier AI securely, and existing frameworks such as RAND's model weight security levels stop at the lab.

We are aiming to convene experts from frontier labs, AI security institutes, think tanks, standard developers and middle-power governments to develop concrete, actionable recommendations on specific issues. It would use RAND's framework as the baseline, update the recommendations periodically as technology, threats and best practices evolve, and feed gaps back to standard developers. Host governments could voluntarily adopt the proto-standards to earn trusted access, while labs and partner governments could draw on or point to them when deciding where frontier models are hosted and on what terms.

Once proto-standards are endorsed, our project would provide technical assistance to help host governments meet them through their own laws and institutions. This explainer walks through our strategy and theory of change, the risks the proto-standards address, what they would cover, how a government would use them under pressure, and how they could be adopted.

The risk

00Hosting frontier AI opens leaks.

Frontier AI will increasingly be hosted in middle powers. Hosting brings two risks that a host government, not a lab, is best placed to manage.

Middle powers can gain leverage by offering secured compute in exchange for access to frontier models, but only if they have adequate standards, trust and enforcement. Labs and partner governments increasingly treat security as the price of access: the more trusted a host, the higher the tier of access it gets (Leicht, 80,000 Hours). Today's standards set what labs and data center operators must do. Nothing yet says what a host government must have in place.

Risk 01

Theft of model weights and algorithms

Exfiltration of model weights or proprietary algorithms through technical, human (insider) or cyber routes, by nation-states or malicious actors.

The proto-standard: vetting, insider-threat programs, inspection authority, security-conditioned licensing and a clearly defined offence in the host country's law.
Risk 02

Chip smuggling

Controlled AI chips diverted through transit hubs using front companies, false end-user papers and payment trails, where domestic law, investigators and prosecutors are not equipped to stop it.

The proto-standard: AI-chip red flag guidance, offences in domestic law that enable investigation and prosecution, and the enforcement capacity to act on leads.

The stakes

Model theft and chip smuggling create a direct path to catastrophic risk

Once frontier weights leave a secure site, every safeguard the developer built can be stripped out: safety training removed, the model fine-tuned for cyberattacks or biological weapons design, and copies run anywhere, with no way to recall them. Diverted chips do the same for capability itself: they let actors train frontier-scale models outside any testing, monitoring or treaty, which undermines the compute-based agreements most proposals for pacing frontier AI rely on. A single leak is irreversible, so prevention has to happen before hosting, not after.
So what for safety

Every frontier model or chip that leaks through a hub ends up outside any safety or verification regime. Security is the prerequisite for hosting frontier AI safely, and for trusted access.

Security first

01Skip security, lose the case.

Governments often reach for enforcement first: a high-profile prosecution, a public warning. Without the foundations underneath, those cases fall apart, and each failure teaches thieves and smugglers where the gaps are.

If we skip the foundations
Cases collapseNo access, no suspect, no usable charge.
The hub looks softThieves and smugglers learn where the gaps are.
Labs hold backThey host without conditions, or not at all.
Partners cut accessHigher tiers of frontier access go to safer hosts.
Leaks continueChips and weights keep flowing outside any safety regime.
So what for safety

Enforcement without the foundations fails, and every failed case makes the next theft more likely. The proto-standard is the foundations.

The fix

02Things only a government can do.

The proto-standard sits on top of lab-side standards such as RAND's security levels (RAND) and the SL5 Task Force standard. It covers only what a host government can do: vetting people, entering sites, licensing and prosecuting.

Theory of change

03Agree the standard, then help hosts meet it.

One main activity, a shared proto-standard, and two lines of technical assistance that help host governments meet it for model weights and for AI chips. What the assistance teaches feeds back into the next version of the standard.

If host governments agree a shared security proto-standard and get help meeting it through their own laws, then frontier weights and AI chips are far less likely to leak through middle powers, because security becomes a condition of trusted access that every actor has reason to meet.
The problem

Hosts cannot yet protect the frontier AI on their soil

No data center meets SL4-SL5; up to 1.6 million H100-equivalents smuggled through 2025; no host-government standard for vetting, site access or AI-chip enforcement.

Who it affects

Host and transit governments first

Security, regulatory and justice agencies; customs and financial intelligence units. Then labs, partners and the public.

The story

An insider copies weights from a foreign-run site

Today: no vetting, no access, no usable charge. With the standard: a report within hours and a case under local law.

The approach

Endorse, then implement

An outside advocate in the style of FATF or Transparency International. Own laws, published no-go conditions.

Inputs

Convenings, technical security partners, AI security institutes, financial intelligence unit networks, RAND and the SL5 Task Force.

Theory of victory · what winning looks like

The win condition

  1. A shared, expert-endorsed host-government security proto-standard exists, and labs and partner governments refer to it when deciding where frontier models are hosted.
  2. At least one major hub visibly meets it, and hosting there is seen as safe rather than a theft route.
  3. AI-chip diversion through hubs is routinely detected and prosecuted under local law.
  4. Because middle powers can verify and enforce, future international agreements to pace or limit frontier AI become enforceable.

Why the actors converge

  • Host governments want trusted access and investment.
  • Labs want hosting options without theft risk.
  • Partner governments want diversion stopped without running enforcement abroad.
  • Security becomes the price of access, so it is in everyone's interest.

Signposts

Winning

  • Labs or partner governments cite the proto-standard.
  • A host designates a lead agency.
  • A prosecution uses the new tools.
  • A tabletop is run jointly by 2+ countries.

Losing

  • Access is decided by political deals with no security conditions.
  • A theft is traced to a hosted site.
  • Standards are seen as Washington's demands rather than shared ones.
Assumptions

What has to hold

  • Host demand: at least two governments want readiness support and will share information in confidence.
  • Acceptance: labs and partner governments accept host-implemented standards as meaningful.
  • Technical depth: a technical security partner covers model-security controls.
  • Access politics: access deals and carveouts for trusted allies continue.
  • Neutral standing: hosts see the effort as an outside advocate, not Washington's enforcer.
Could this really happen?

Yes. Every actor gets something it already wants, whether trusted access, safer hosting or diversion stopped. The government's cost is designations, amendments and training, not new infrastructure, and the first products need no new law.

The games

04Now play it through.

Two short games put the proto-standard under pressure. First, run ten AI-server shipments through a middle-power hub and see how many get through. Then step into Singapore's shoes and respond to a theft and a smuggling case with the laws it actually has.

Part one

Run the shipments

Ten shipments carrying AI servers try to slip through a middle-power hub, each running a scheme drawn from public cases. The checkpoints are chances to catch a scheme, not places on a map. Watch what gets through today, then add the proto-standard one measure at a time.

Now you try

Switch any measure on or off, then run the shipments. Sources for the schemes: DOJ, BBC, FDD, CSET.

How this works
  • Each scheme leaves traces at one or more checkpoints (the rings). Each measure works at one checkpoint. A shipment stops at the first checkpoint where it leaves a trace and a measure is switched on, so one measure can catch several schemes.
  • The checkpoints are chances to catch a scheme, not places on a map: a bank payment and an export declaration happen in the same hub.
  • Two catches exist today without new measures: a false final destination can be caught by existing destination declarations, and one shipment is caught after delivery through a tip-off, as in a real case. That two-in-ten rate is close to Epoch's assumption that about a quarter of diversion is detected (Epoch AI).
  • Real schemes adapt, so no standard catches everything for long. The schemes are illustrative patterns from the cited cases, not data, and the counts are not probabilities.
So what for safety

Today, most diverted chips sail through a hub, and the few that are caught are caught late. With the proto-standard, most are stopped before they leave, while there is still time to keep frontier compute inside a safety regime.

How this works
  • Each scenario has three decisions with three possible moves. The strongest moves need specific laws at a set level; if your laws fall short, the move is locked and says what is missing.
  • Moves score 0 to 3. A total of 8 or 9 gives the best ending, 5 to 7 the middle ending, and 4 or less the worst.

So what for safety

Singapore already has strong tools against chip diversion. Its gaps are in vetting, site access, AI-chip red flags and passing leads to neighbors, which is exactly where the proto-standard turns a late, strained response into a contained one.
