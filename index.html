00

The risk

Safety impact

Frontier AI is moving into middle powers. Hosting it opens two leak routes: theft of model weights from data centers on local soil, and diversion of AI chips through trading hubs. Either one puts frontier capability in the hands of actors outside any safety regime.

290k-1.6MH100-equivalent chips estimated smuggled to China through 2025, up to a third of its AI computeEpoch AI, 2026
0data centers worldwide meeting the security level recommended for frontier hosting (SL4-SL5)KIRA Center, 2025
78%of surveyed security experts expect a state to steal frontier weights from a U.S. company before 2030 (n=27)AI 2027 security forecast
~50%of data center projects outside the U.S. are operated by U.S. companies, so hosting a site is not the same as controlling itRichardson et al., 2025

Singapore sits on both routes. It hosts more than 1.6 gigawatts of data center capacity and wants to be a regional AI hub (MDDI, 2026). It is also one of the world's busiest trade and finance centers, which is why prosecutors there have already charged people over servers that likely contained export-controlled Nvidia chips (BBC, 2026).

Labs and partner governments increasingly treat security as the price of access: the more trusted a host, the higher the tier of frontier access it gets (Leicht, 80,000 Hours). Today's standards set what labs and data center operators must do. Nothing yet says what a host government must have in place. This page proposes that missing piece, and tests it against Singapore.

So what for safetyEvery frontier model or chip that leaks through a hub ends up outside any safety or verification regime. Security is the prerequisite for hosting frontier AI safely and for trusted access.

How this works: the rules behind the meters
  • Detection hours = internal flag delay + reporting delay. Card 2 (insider program): Full 0h, Partial +4h, Off +12h. Card 7 (incident reporting): Full +2h, Partial +12h, Off +36h (detection relies on the operator volunteering it).
  • Prosecution: No if Card 5 (law) is Off, or if Cards 1 and 3 are both Off (no evidence). Yes if Card 5 is Full and Card 1 or 3 is Full. Otherwise Weak.
  • Trust penalty: +1 if detection over 6h; +1 more if over 24h; Card 8 Off +1, Partial +0.5; Card 4 Off +0.5, Partial +0.25; prosecution No +1, Weak +0.5.
  • Partner trust: penalty under 1 = Kept; under 3.5 = Strained; 3.5 or more = Access cut.
  • Endings follow partner trust: Kept = "Contained, access kept"; Strained = "Contained late, access strained"; Access cut = "Weights gone, access cut".
  • These are simple illustrative rules we chose, not predictions. Card 6 (chips) does not affect this weights scenario.

So what for safetyWhether a theft ends contained or with access cut is decided by rules written before the night it happens: who must report, who can enter the site, and who tells partners.

05

The fix

Safety impact

A short host-government security proto-standard, a theory of change for getting it adopted, and a picture of what winning looks like.

5a. The v0 proto-standard v0 strawman for expert review

Eight requirements for any government that hosts frontier model weights or sits on AI-chip trade routes. They sit on top of lab-side standards such as RAND's security levels (RAND) and the SL5 Task Force standard, and cover what only a government can do.

5b. Theory of change

How the project produces change. Filter by pathway; the dashed accountability line separates what the project delivers from what it contributes to.

1 · The problem

Host and transit governments cannot yet protect the frontier AI on their soil

No data center meets SL4-SL5; up to 1.6 million H100-equivalents smuggled to China through 2025; no agency clearly owns AI incidents; lab-side frameworks cover labs, not host governments, and not smuggling.

2 · Who it affects

Host and transit governments first

Direct: security, regulatory and justice agencies; prosecutors, customs and financial intelligence units; standard developers. Indirect: labs and partner governments; the AI safety field; global society.

3 · The story

An insider copies weights from a foreign-run site

Today: no vetting, no clear inspection power, no one tells partners; access is cut. With the standard: vetted staff, a report within hours, investigation under local law, partners alerted; access continues.

4 · The approach

Government-side implementation no one else owns

Endorse, then implement. An outside advocate in the style of FATF or Transparency International, not an auditor. Each country uses its own laws and keeps its own access. Neutral on buildout, with published no-go conditions.

5 · Inputs

What goes in

Convenings, technical security partners, AI security institutes, financial intelligence unit networks, RAND and the SL5 Task Force.

5c. Theory of victory

The theory of change is the causal path. The theory of victory is what winning looks like, why the key actors would converge on it, and how we would know we are winning or losing.

What winning looks like

  1. A shared, expert-endorsed host-government security proto-standard exists, and labs and partner governments refer to it when deciding where frontier models are hosted.
  2. At least one major hub (Singapore) visibly meets it, and hosting there is seen as safe rather than a theft route.
  3. AI-chip diversion through hubs is routinely detected and prosecuted under local law.
  4. A serious AI incident triggers a report within hours and partner alerts, not misattribution between rivals.
  5. Because middle powers can verify and enforce, future international agreements to pace or limit frontier AI become enforceable.

Why the actors converge

  • Host governments want trusted access and investment.
  • Labs want hosting options without theft risk.
  • Partner governments want diversion stopped without running enforcement themselves.
  • Security becomes the price of access, so it is in everyone's interest.

Signposts

Winning

  • Labs or partner governments cite the proto-standard.
  • A host designates a lead agency.
  • A prosecution uses the new tools.
  • A tabletop is run jointly by 2+ countries.

Losing

  • Access is decided by political deals with no security conditions.
  • A theft is traced to a hosted site.
  • Standards are seen as Washington's demands rather than shared ones.

So what for safetyThe proto-standard closes the two leak routes at the host, and the same institutions are what any future agreement to pace or limit frontier AI would need to be enforceable.

Cost to the government

Qualitative only, and modest:

  • Regulatory amendments to existing laws (data center licensing scope, a prescribed incident type, a weights offence).
  • A designated lead agency for AI incidents with catastrophic potential.
  • Training for customs, police, prosecutors and financial intelligence analysts.
  • Reporting rules and a partner contact chain.
  • No new infrastructure.

What Singapore already has

  • Fraud and money-laundering prosecutions in the AI-server case, with a SGD 55 million house seized (Malay Mail).
  • The Strategic Goods (Control) Act and an April 2025 AI-chip advisory (Customs).
  • Two-hour incident reporting for critical information infrastructure (CSA).
  • A Digital Infrastructure Bill licensing large data centers, passed 7 October 2026 (Fintech News Singapore).
  • An AI Safety Institute and a frontier-model testing partnership (Singapore AISI; TechNode Global).
  • A CSA advisory on frontier AI cyber risks, April 2026 (Baker McKenzie).

Risks and mitigations

  • Seen as enforcing for Washington. Mitigated by own-law framing (fraud, money laundering, computer misuse), host co-chairs and co-authored outputs.
  • "More hosting means more theft surface." Mitigated by published no-go conditions, so a readiness assessment can say a site is not ready.
  • Technical credibility. Mitigated by partnering with model-security experts for the technical layer.
  • Access to security services. Work through AI security institutes, interior and justice ministries and financial intelligence units.

What the project pursues

The interventions inside the four pathways: core work, the methods used to deliver it, and one small add-on.

Could this really happen?Yes. The step up is mainly amendments, designations and training on top of laws Singapore already has, and every actor gets something it already wants: trusted access, safer hosting, or diversion stopped.

07

What to build now

Feasibility

A phased 24-month path. Each phase produces something a government can use within months.

Months 0-6

Convene and draft

  • Convene labs, officials and researchers; draft the proto-standard.
  • Legal gap map of offences prosecutors can already use, for 5-6 jurisdictions.
  • Red-flag guidance for chips, for banks, financial intelligence units and freight forwarders.
  • First round of dialogues and roundtables in pilot countries.
Months 6-12

Endorse and assess

  • Endorse proto-standard v1 through the convenings.
  • First readiness assessment and action plan in a host country.
  • First national incident tabletop.
Months 12-24

Implement and connect

  • Hosts act on their plans: vetting, insider-threat programs, inspection procedures.
  • Guidance adopted by a financial intelligence unit network.
  • Cross-border incident protocol tested in 2+ countries.

Signposts

Month 3Pilot countries chosen; 25+ structured conversations with labs, AI security institutes and financial intelligence units.If not: narrow to chip-smuggling enforcement.
Month 6Legal gap map and red flags delivered; one host requests a readiness assessment.If not: go, adjust or stop.
Month 12Proto-standard endorsed; first readiness assessment and incident tabletop done.If not: refocus.
Month 242+ hosts acting on plans; 40-60 officials in the network; incident protocol tested across 2+ countries.Then: decide the next phase.

Could this really happen?The first useful products (a legal gap map and red flags) take months, not years, and need no new law. Bigger changes follow only once governments have endorsed the standard.

Key debates

Align with Washington or hedge?Some analysts argue middle powers should align closely with the U.S. to keep access; others argue that pressure to pick a bloc makes hedging more rational.
Is more frontier capacity in more countries good?Balance-of-power arguments say yes; others see more actors and more copies of weights as more risk.
Who sets security standards?Some expect the U.S. to set them with carveouts for trusted allies; others note that buyers resist conditions set by one supplier country.
Do export controls reduce risk?Some see enforcement as part of a race; others see it as the first step of any deterrence or treaty regime; some doubt controls slow the frontier at all.
What are AI summits for?Some want summits to deliver incident reporting and verification; others think they suit only information sharing and narrow deals.
Is verification near or far?Verifying great-power commitments is widely seen as hard; verifying a host's domestic compliance is a lower bar that may be reachable now.

So whatThe field broadly agrees on the problem and argues about strategy. Host-side security institutions are useful under most of the strategies on offer.

About and methods

Clarity

Snapshot

This page reflects public sources available as of 30 October 2026, published as a November 2026 snapshot. Laws and cases cited here may change; several are ongoing.

The scenario is hypothetical

"Weights Walk Out" is invented. No real lab, operator or company is depicted as a victim or culprit.

How ratings were assigned

In place: a law or institution clearly meets the requirement. Partial: something exists but does not fully cover frontier weights or AI chips. Gap: sources show nothing meets it. Unclear: we could not confirm either way from public sources. Ratings rely on public sources only, including law-firm summaries; they are not legal advice.

How the meters work

The tabletop and sequencing meters run on the simple, visible rules listed under each interactive. They illustrate how the requirements interact; they are not predictions or probabilities.
